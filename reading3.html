Reading 2

Reflection: The graphic novel Motherhood in Crisis by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager, and Saidu Bah was interesting, but honestly I do not fully understand what it has to do with a visual design class. I understand that visual storytelling can communicate social issues and emotions, and graphic novels are obviously a visual medium, but the message itself felt more sociological and political than design-focused to me. The article mainly focused on the pressures, expectations, and realities surrounding motherhood, especially how society treats motherhood almost like a requirement instead of a personal choice. One thing I did connect with was the idea that society and media constantly push the “mother” agenda onto women. That is something I have always noticed even outside of this article. From a young age, people are already expected to build their future around marriage and children, almost like it is a mandatory life path. People literally schedule having kids into their life plans the same way they would schedule college or a career. There is this assumption that every woman will eventually become a mother and that motherhood automatically equals fulfillment or success. The graphic novel reflected a lot of that pressure and how overwhelming it can become. At the same time, I do think motherhood can be a beautiful thing, but only if someone genuinely wants it. I think that choice matters more than society admits. The article made me think about how often people romanticize motherhood without actually talking about the responsibilities that come with it. I know people within Nigerian culture who have had very large families, even up to eleven children, and still do not know how to properly take care of other people emotionally or responsibly. Having children does not automatically make someone nurturing, mature, or capable. That is why I think society focuses too much on the idea of becoming a mother rather than the actual responsibility of parenting itself. Visually, I can understand why the creators chose the graphic novel format because it allows emotional topics to feel more personal and immersive. The illustrations and storytelling help communicate stress, isolation, and pressure in ways plain text sometimes cannot. Graphic novels can simplify complicated social issues into something visually engaging and easier to emotionally process. Even though I personally did not strongly connect the work to visual design as a class topic, I can still see how the combination of imagery and narrative was used to strengthen the message. Overall, the article mainly reinforced ideas I already believed rather than changing my perspective. It reminded me how deeply society pushes motherhood expectations onto women and how important personal choice is in that conversation. For me, motherhood should never be treated like an obligation or a required achievement. It only becomes meaningful if it is genuinely wanted and supported by the ability to care for another person responsibly.
