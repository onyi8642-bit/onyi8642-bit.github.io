Extra Credit · Nancy Baker Cahill

Artist Talk Reflection · April 29th, The Black Box, HN543

Nancy Baker Cahill

Reflection

Nancy Baker Cahill’s artist talk was interesting to me because it challenged what I think art can be, even though I did not personally connect to most of her work emotionally. I did not dislike it either. I actually respected her a lot as an artist, especially because of her journey and the way she thinks about creating. What stood out to me the most was not necessarily the final artwork itself, but the process behind it, the technology she uses, and the way she combines so many different fields together. She described herself almost like a trans-disciplinary artist where there are no clear separations between mediums, technology, science, activism, and art. That idea connects strongly to topics we discussed in class about how modern media artists blur boundaries between disciplines. One thing that immediately interested me was the fact that she is self-taught. She talked about feeling self-conscious around traditional artists, especially because she started with graphite drawing before eventually moving into VR, AR, rendering, projection mapping, and photogrammetry. I think that journey is inspiring because it shows how artists can evolve into completely different forms over time. Drawing was still the foundation of her work though, and she mentioned that everything begins with drawing for her. That stood out to me because even with all the advanced technology she uses now, the core creative process still begins traditionally. It reminded me that technology does not replace artistic fundamentals; it just expands them. Another thing I noticed was how sculptural and spatial her work felt. Even though some of her projects exist digitally or through AR, she talked about them almost like physical environments. She focused heavily on scale, distortions, VFX, movement, sound, and the feeling of existing in multiple spaces at once. She described AR as operating in both visible and invisible spaces simultaneously, almost relating it to ideas from quantum mechanics. That concept was probably the most interesting part of the lecture to me because it pushed art beyond something static hanging on a wall. Her work becomes immersive and active instead of passive. Projection mapping and inverted AR images especially reflected principles of space, movement, scale, and perception that we have discussed in class. She also connected her work strongly to civic engagement and ecological awareness. She spoke about projects involving oceans, AI, and even the LA wildfires. It was clear that she wants her work to carry strong messages about environmental and social issues. She mentioned taking part in an oceans and AI course at Harvard, which showed how analytical her creative process is. I noticed that she constantly balances making art with researching ideas, securing funding, and building platforms for public interaction. She also discussed creating anti-gatekeeping AI platforms and making art accessible in new ways. That was another important theme from the lecture because it showed how contemporary artists are not only creators but also organizers, researchers, technologists, and communicators. What the lecture meant to me personally is more complicated. I respected her ideas and found her journey genuinely interesting, but emotionally her work did not spark much in me. I think her work is impressive technically and conceptually, but at the same time I found it overly explanatory. A picture should tell a thousand words on its own. I personally believe that one of the reasons everyone can be considered an artist is because art only needs to spark something in someone, even if that person is the artist themselves. Any medium can become art if it creates emotion, thought, curiosity, or connection. For me, a lot of Nancy Baker Cahill’s work depended heavily on explanation and context before it became meaningful. The fact that each piece required such detailed explanations made me feel like the artwork itself could not fully communicate the story independently. I am not criticizing her for that because honestly my own work can be similar. I sometimes rely too much on concepts or explanations too. The difference is that I am personally not satisfied with that in my own art. Listening to her speak, I got the impression that she is comfortable with art functioning that way, and there is nothing wrong with that. It is just not what emotionally connects with me as an artist or viewer. I prefer work that immediately creates feeling or meaning visually before words are needed. Her work felt very intellectual and analytical, while I usually connect more to art that feels instinctive or emotionally immediate. At the same time, the lecture still impacted me because it expanded my perspective on what art can involve. Even though I did not emotionally connect to the work itself, I connected to the idea of experimentation and the lack of boundaries between mediums. Seeing someone move from graphite drawing into VR and AR reminded me that artists do not have to stay confined to one material or method forever. Her work reinforced the idea that technology itself can become a medium just like paint, clay, or charcoal. That is probably the biggest thing I took away from the talk. I may not personally connect to her style of storytelling, but I respect the innovation, ambition, and thought behind it.
